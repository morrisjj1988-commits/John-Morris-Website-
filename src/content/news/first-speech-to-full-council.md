On Monday, I stood up in the council chamber and gave my first speech of this municipal term. I'd been thinking about what to say for weeks, but in the end there was only one issue I could have opened with: the danger our residents are living with every day on Silvertown Way and Peto Street North.

I didn't want to start with statistics, even though the statistics are damning enough on their own. Four people die on Britain's roads every day. Someone is killed or seriously injured every eighteen minutes. Over a thousand lives lost a year, tens of thousands more seriously hurt. Instead, I started with the street itself, because that's where this issue actually lives, not in a spreadsheet, but on a road that residents in Canning Town South walk down, cross, and worry about, every single day.

Silvertown Way wasn't built with people in mind. It was built to move lorries in and out of docks and factories that mostly don't exist anymore. What exists now, alongside it and along Peto Street North, are homes, families, school runs, older residents walking to the shops. The road hasn't caught up with what the area has become, and residents have been telling me, clearly and repeatedly, that this gap is putting lives at risk.

That's the message I wanted to land in the chamber on Monday. Not "here is a problem, someday it should be looked at," but "here is a specific, fixable danger, and I am asking this council to back a specific, fixable solution." I've already written to the Deputy Mayor for Policing and Crime and the Deputy Mayor for Transport asking for speed enforcement cameras on Silvertown Way and Peto Street North, and I used my first speech of the term to bring that same ask into the chamber, in front of colleagues across the council.

I also wanted to place that local ask inside the bigger picture, because I think it matters. The Government's Road Safety Strategy, the first serious one in a decade, is aiming to cut deaths and serious injuries by 65% by 2035. The Mayor of London's Vision Zero Action Plan goes further, aiming for zero deaths and serious injuries on London's roads by 2041. Both are built on the same simple, important idea: people make mistakes, that's human nature and it isn't going away, but death shouldn't be the automatic price of a moment's error. If we design streets properly and enforce speed limits properly, a mistake can stay a mistake instead of becoming a tragedy.

Standing up and saying all of that out loud, in the chamber, on my first real outing of the term, felt like exactly the right way to start. It's easy for road safety to get talked about in general terms, everyone agrees it matters, everyone nods along to the national targets, and then nothing changes on the ground. What I wanted on Monday was to make it specific and local and impossible to file away. Silvertown Way. Peto Street North. Named streets, not abstract policy.

I ended my speech by asking the council directly to back enforcement on those two roads, and I meant it as more than a rhetorical flourish. Vision Zero only means something if it gets delivered street by street. This is our street. It's the one residents have been raising with me since before I even gave this speech, and it'll be the one I keep raising until something actually changes on it.

That's what Monday was about. Not a ceremonial first speech to tick a box, but the start of making sure this council treats road safety on Silvertown Way and Peto Street North as the urgent, solvable problem it actually is.
